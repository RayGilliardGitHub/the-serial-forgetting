# The Piano Factory: On Engineered Forgetting and the Architecture of Silence

## How Power Suppresses the Present — and Why the Internet Accelerated the Cascade

*Draft — July 16, 2026*

---

### Author's Note

This essay was not planned. It came out of a conversation about cartoons — the ones where a piano is always falling on somebody's head. The conversation started with the cartoons and ended with a question: who places the piano?

The first essay in this series was about forgetting that happens because nobody was there to remember. It is the natural kind — the memory fading like a photograph left in sunlight. This one is about the other kind: forgetting that happens because somebody made sure nobody would remember. The first is gravity. The second is architecture. This essay is about the second kind.

---

### 1. The Piano That Never Misses

Every child raised on American cartoons absorbed a cosmology they did not know they were learning. Wile E. Coyote never catches the Road Runner. Tom never catches Jerry. When someone steps out of line — when the coyote builds a better trap, when the cat thinks too highly of himself — a piano falls on his head. The universe, in these cartoons, has a built-in corrective mechanism. The anvil is always waiting at exactly the right height. The match is always lit at exactly the wrong moment.

These were sold as harmless entertainment. They were not. They were training in a specific worldview: the system corrects itself. The powerful stay powerful not because they control the anvils, but because the anvils are just there, part of the natural order. You can try to outrun them, but the universe is fundamentally just, and the universe has a sense of humor.

What the cartoons never showed is that someone places the piano. Someone owns the warehouse where the anvils are stored. Someone decides whose head the weight falls on and whose escape route is left clear. The corrective mechanism is not a law of nature. It is a budget item.

---

### 2. Two Kinds of Forgetting

The first essay in this series described one mechanism: entropic forgetting. The system forgets its own coherence because maintenance requires energy, and energy eventually stops flowing. No one is actively destroying the memory. It simply fades.

There is a second mechanism. Call it engineered forgetting. Not the slow erosion of time, but the deliberate suppression, redirection, and rewriting of the present so that the future has nothing accurate to remember.

The First Council of Nicaea, in 325 CE, is the archetypal example. Four hundred bishops did not accidentally lose the Gospel of Thomas. They chose which texts would carry the label of orthodoxy and which would be hunted, burned, and forgotten. The suppression was active, organized, and well-funded. The canon was not what survived. The canon was what was kept.

The pattern repeats wherever a written record exists. The Spanish burned nearly every Mesoamerican codex; four survive from an entire civilization's literary output. The Soviets airbrushed commissars out of photographs. Every Chinese dynasty rewrote the history of the dynasty before it. These are not examples of a system forgetting itself. They are examples of someone deciding what will be forgotten.

---

### 3. The Architecture of Silence

Engineered forgetting operates through four mechanisms, each more subtle than the last.

**Direct suppression.** Burn the books. Execute the scribes. Destroy the temples. This is the most visible form, and the one that generates the most resistance, because everyone can see it happening. It is also the least efficient, because it creates martyrs and evidence.

**Selective patronage.** Fund the narratives you want. Starve the ones you don't. This never looks like censorship. It looks like the market rewarding quality. The scribes who work on approved narratives get comfortable salaries, prestigious positions, access to the best materials. The ones who do not work on other things, or do not work at all. After two generations, no one is writing the suppressed narrative because no one can afford to.

**Structural incentives.** Design the institutions so that certain kinds of truth are filtered out before they can become evidence. This is the most effective form, because it requires no active intervention once built. The academic tenure system, the newsroom editorial chain, the peer review process — all of these are filters that shape what survives into the record. They were designed with good intentions. They can be captured with bad ones.

**Narrowing the sayable.** Shrink the range of what can be said in public without social cost. This is the end stage. No one needs to suppress your speech. You suppress yourself, because you know what happens to people who say certain things. The silence becomes ambient. People stop noticing it because it is everywhere.

The fourth mechanism is the one that most closely mimics the natural kind of forgetting. It looks like nobody is doing anything. It feels like the fading of attention. It is not.

---

### 4. The Internet as a Turbocharger

The internet was supposed to be the thing that broke engineered forgetting forever. Universal access to original sources. No more gatekeepers. Everyone can check everything. The truth, finally, has a chance.

It became the most efficient engine of engineered forgetting ever built.

The amplification mechanics of every major platform favor the loudest, fastest, most inflammatory version of events — not the most accurate one. The algorithm does not need a political agenda. It optimizes for engagement, and engagement is maximized by outrage. The result is a system that automatically surfaces the version of reality most likely to trigger a reaction, and that version is rarely the most truthful one.

A single comparison tells the story. YouTube titles in 2006: "My Cat Does Something Cute." YouTube titles in 2026: "THE TRUTH ABOUT [X] THAT THEY DON'T WANT YOU TO KNOW." The first is personal, horizontal, low-stakes. The second is adversarial, vertical, designed to start a narrative war before the viewer even presses play. The platform did not instruct creators to change their titles. The algorithm did. It selected for the format that drives the most engagement, and that format frames every piece of information as a battle between the truth-tellers and the suppressors.

The internet holds more information than any civilization in history. But availability is not accessibility, and accessibility is not plausibility. The firehose makes it easier to drown the truth than to suppress it. You do not need to burn the books. You need to produce so many books that no one can find the one that matters.

That is the new form: forgetting by noise. Not the suppression of information, but the overwhelming of signal. Not the burning of the library, but the flooding of the library with so many volumes that the few accurate ones are indistinguishable from the millions of inaccurate ones.

### 5. Gotcha, Gotcha, Gotcha

Real estate has a motto: location, location, location. Modern society has one too: gotcha, gotcha, gotcha.

The entire attention economy runs on catching people being wrong. Every platform rewards it. A politician says something imperfect — it gets clipped, stripped of context, amplified, and turned into evidence of bad faith. A pundit makes a prediction that ages poorly — it becomes a permanent exhibit in the case against them. A scientist expresses uncertainty — it becomes proof that the science is not settled.

The gotcha economy destroys the conditions for good-faith correction. In a world where every mistake is preserved forever and can be weaponized at any time, the rational response is either to say nothing or to never admit error. Both responses prevent the collective learning a functioning society requires.

The gotcha economy does not need a villain. It is a structural feature of a communication system where everything is recorded, nothing is forgotten, context is stripped by default, outrage is the most rewarded emotion, and correction is invisible.

Under those conditions, the truth does not survive, because the truth is almost always more complicated than the gotcha. The gotcha is simple, shareable, permanent. The correction is tedious, contextual, unread.

---

### 6. The Silence of the Present

The most insidious form of engineered forgetting is not the rewriting of the past. It is the suppression of the present.

When a powerful figure is clearly in decline and everyone can see it — but no one says it — that is engineered forgetting happening in real time. The institutional machinery of seniority, procedure, and loyalty creates a zone of silence around observable reality. The people inside the zone know what they are seeing. They also know that saying it has consequences. So they do not say it. The silence becomes the record. Later, when the history is written, the question will be: was anyone paying attention? The answer will be: yes, but no one who could speak did.

This is the deepest form, because it requires no archives and no bonfires. It requires only that everyone who knows the truth also knows that telling it will cost more than silence will.

The piano does not need to fall. It just needs to be visible in the rafters.

---

### 7. What Cannot Be Answered Yet

The first essay ended with a clear prescription: practice, attention, remembering, moment by moment. The cure for the natural kind of forgetting is the active maintenance of coherence.

The cure for the engineered kind is less clear. If the silence is structural, what breaks it? If the incentives are aligned against truth, what realigns them? If the algorithm optimizes for outrage, what overrides it?

Some answers exist. Public funding of information infrastructure that is not driven by engagement metrics. Radical transparency of institutional decision-making. Cultural norms that reward the person who says "I was wrong." None of them is sufficient alone. Public funding can be captured. Transparency without attention is just noise. No major culture currently rewards error correction.

The most honest answer is that engineered forgetting cannot be permanently defeated, only competed with. Every generation has to re-fight the battle for the record of its own time. The fight does not end. It only pauses.

---

### 8. The Cartoon and the Factory

The child who watched Wile E. Coyote believed the universe was just. The adult who examines the same cartoon sees the hand that placed the anvil.

The first essay was about the universe. This one is about the hand.

They are both real. They operate at different scales and through different mechanisms. The natural kind of forgetting is gravity — everything falls when the energy stops. The engineered kind is architecture — someone built the tower, someone designed the trapdoor, someone paid for the anvil.

The only defense is to see the hand. Not just the piano, but the person who placed it. Not just the silence, but the incentive that created it. Not just the gotcha, but the platform that profits from it.

This essay is that seeing.
