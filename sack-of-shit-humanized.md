# Sack of Shit: Entropy, Meaning, and the Temporary Membrane

## An Essay in One Session — Revised After Feedback

*Draft — July 18, 2026*

---

### Author's Note

This essay is different from the others in this series. *The Serial Forgetting* was constructed over weeks, revised, published. *The Piano Factory* was a single sitting but revised after. This one is being written directly from a single conversation — one long session that started with a news article about a parasite and ended with the Bhagavad Gita, the Fermi Paradox, and the question of whether any of this matters.

It may read like what it is: a mind working through things in real time. I have chosen not to sand off the edges.

As with the earlier essays, the reader is cautioned against absolutes. When I say "the universe does not care," I am not claiming to know the mind of the universe, or that it has one. I am describing what the evidence suggests: that the operations of physics proceed without reference to human preference. When I discuss what a baby does or does not experience, I am making an observation about observable behavior — not claiming access to an internal state. The pattern of overreach is the same one I have corrected before, and I have tried to catch it this time.

---

### 1. The Parasite That Started It

On July 16, 2026, the FDA announced an investigation into an outbreak of Cyclospora cayetanensis across five states. The parasite was traced to shredded iceberg lettuce from Mexico, supplied by Taylor Farms de Mexico in Guanajuato, served at Taco Bell. Over 1,600 confirmed cases. Nearly 7,000 suspected.

Cyclospora causes explosive, watery diarrhea that can persist for weeks without treatment. It is not life-threatening for most people, but it is devastating for someone who cannot afford to miss work. Since this essay was written, the outbreak has claimed two lives. The CDC reported the first deaths on August 3, 2026 — both in Michigan, both people with underlying health conditions. Not life-threatening, until it is. And the people who eat at Taco Bell are disproportionately people who cannot afford to miss work.

The parasite has a simple life cycle. It lives in human intestines. Infected humans shed it in their feces. If that feces gets into water, and that water irrigates lettuce, and that lettuce is not cooked, the parasite reaches a new host. There is no animal reservoir. The CDC states flatly: "Humans appear to be the only major host." When Cyclospora oocysts appear in animal stool, it is because the animal ate human feces and the parasite passed through.

The contamination is not from animals. It is from humans. Specifically: human sewage in the irrigation water or on the hands of field workers who lack access to toilets and handwashing stations. It is a sanitation infrastructure failure in an endemic region, repeating a pattern documented since the 1990s — Guatemalan raspberries in 1996, Mexican cilantro in 2013, Mexican basil in 2019, and now Mexican iceberg lettuce in 2026.

The first lawsuits were filed within 24 hours. Marler Clark, the same firm that took on Jack in the Box after the 1993 E. coli outbreak, filed *Ayyad v. Pacific Bells, LLC* in federal court in Ohio. A second suit named Taylor Farms directly.

I found myself reading the news and thinking about a CDC lab that had been cut from 11 people to 3. About a surveillance program that no longer required states to report Cyclospora. About 3,800 FDA employees who were no longer there to inspect anything. These are political facts, not philosophical ones. I do not know how to prove that the cuts caused the outbreak to be worse than it would have been. I suspect they did. That suspicion lives in a different part of my mind than the parts that usually write essays. I mention it here because it is part of what set this session in motion, not because I can prove the causal chain.

What I can say with certainty: the lettuce was contaminated by human feces. The company that sold it has a documented history of contamination from the same region. And the people who got sick are disproportionately the ones who could least afford to get sick. Those are facts.

### 2. The Room with My Sister

I know some of the 77 million people who voted for the administration that made those cuts personally. They are my brother and my two sisters, and the people they married.

I visited them around the 2024 election. I had to cut my stay short with one sister in South Carolina. Her and her family were just spouting off stuff about Democrats, Biden, Harris, and others that were not true. And no amount of evidence would change their mind.

I remember sitting at their kitchen table. The TV was on in the background — some cable channel I don't watch. My sister was talking about something she'd seen online. Her husband was nodding. I tried to offer a different number, a different source, a different timeline. It didn't matter. Every fact I offered was already classified in their minds as enemy propaganda. I could see it happening. I could not stop it.

I left telling her to never contact me ever again if they needed help. Because they were too stupid — maybe too indoctrinated — maybe brainwashed by their own ignorance.

I drove home in silence. I don't remember what I was thinking. I remember the silence.

That silence has stayed with me longer than any argument we had. It's still here. I am not sure whether I still mean what I said. I know I meant it then. But that's the thing about anger — it's certain in the moment and uncertain in retrospect.

I do not know whether my sister is stupid. I know that if I had been raised in her town, her church, her media diet, her social circle — I do not know whether I would think differently. The information ecosystem she lives in is designed to be sealed. Every time someone outside it presents contradictory evidence, that evidence gets pre-labeled as untrustworthy. The mechanism is not that she lacks intelligence. The mechanism is that rejecting the tribe's beliefs would mean losing her community, her news source, her identity. People will believe a comfortable lie before they will accept a truth that isolates them.

That is not an excuse. It is an explanation. And the difference between those two things matters more than most people admit.

### 3. The Thousand-Year Pattern

The Bhagavad Gita is a conversation on a battlefield. Arjuna looks at the army he is about to fight and sees his own family, teachers, and friends. He asks Krishna: how can I do this? Krishna answers not by promising victory but by telling Arjuna that he has a right to perform his duty but not to control its outcome. The action itself is the only thing he owns.

That text is 2,500 years old. It was written by a civilization that understood the dynamics of control, duty, and forgetting at least as well as we do.

There is a line in the Diamond Sutra I keep coming back to. Subhuti asks the Buddha whether a person who hears even a single phrase of the teaching can awaken. The Buddha answers: without a doubt. Such a person has planted the seeds of goodness and merit before hundreds of thousands of Buddhas; when the words are heard, a pure faith and clarity awaken in their mind. The greatness of the person who remembers what is not here today but lives somewhere in the future. The same idea recurs across the Gita: the wise see beyond time, beyond birth and death. They see the pattern that others miss because they are too embedded in the moment to notice the shape of the whole.

Every civilization has produced these texts. Every civilization has watched them be weaponized by the institutions that claimed to preserve them. The Spanish burned nearly every Mesoamerican codex; four survive from an entire civilization's literary output. The Soviet Union rewrote its history every decade, airbrushing commissars out of photographs. Every Chinese dynasty rewrote the history of the preceding dynasty to justify its own legitimacy.

The pattern I am describing is not unique to any political party. It is not a recent invention. It is a repeating feature of complex hierarchical systems, as predictable as rust on iron. The technology of control changes. The goal does not.

---

### 4. The Prison We Do Not See

If the pattern is old and the technology is new, the question is whether there is any way out.

The science fiction writers may have been more honest than the historians. *The Matrix*, *The Prisoner*, *Brave New World* — they all describe the same structure: a cage that its inhabitants do not recognize as a cage. The bars are made of beliefs, not steel. The guards are internalized norms, not armed men.

Star Wars presents itself as a story about **good** rebels fighting an **evil** empire. But there's another reading: the rebellion forces the Empire to tighten its grip. Every successful rebel attack is an excuse for more TIE fighters, more stormtroopers, more surveillance. Palpatine needs the rebellion to justify the military state. Without the scattered resistance, the Imperial Senate might have held some power. The rebels might be the useful idiots who hand the Emperor the excuse he needs to dissolve the Senate permanently.

I do not know whether that reading is correct. I do not know whether there is ever a form of resistance the system cannot absorb and repurpose. I find the question interesting. That is all I can honestly say about it.

---

### 5. Entropy Does Not Care

The atom that makes up my desk does not care about the Cyclospora outbreak. The atom that makes up the parasite does not care about the person it infects. The atom that makes up the person does not care about the election. None of it cares. The universe operates by cause and effect, and cause and effect have no opinions.

Entropy is not cruel. Cruelty requires intent. Entropy is just the statistical inevitability of everything moving toward disorder. The fact that we call it "disorder" is already us imposing meaning on a process that has no opinion about it.

The star that goes nova and blasts a planet with gamma rays, wiping out all life on the planet — it is not thinking. It is not angry. It is not punishing anyone. It is just what happens when gravity squeezes too hard and fusion can no longer hold. Cause and effect, all the way down.

There is no end goal. The atoms do not care. The parasites do not have a mind — they have DNA that encodes a response to their environment. Survival of the fittest is not a goal. It is a description of what did not die yet. Nothing in the laws of physics says we need to populate the universe. It is an accident that we exist at all. Some random chemicals four billion years ago happened to get together and do something interesting.

Only humans gave them meaning. We are the part of the universe that became aware of itself. We are the part that can look at entropy and call it a cruel joke — which means we are the part that understands cruelty, jokes, and meaning.

Meaning is not discovered. It is generated.

---

### 6. Where the Aliens Are

I ended *The Serial Forgetting* with a conjecture about the Fermi Paradox: that the silence of the cosmos is not because intelligent life is rare, but because every technological civilization encounters the same forgetting cascade, and few survive it long enough to reach the stars.

I am less sure of that conclusion now.

Maybe the problem is not that civilizations destroy themselves. Maybe the problem is that when a civilization looks at the universe long enough — sees the entropy, sees the indifference, sees that nothing cares, and it runs out of reasons to continue. Not through war or collapse. Through a quiet, collective shrug.

A civilization that figures out it is just a temporary chemical arrangement (a brief local decrease in entropy) has two paths. One is to generate its own meaning, write its own story, keep going because why not. The other is to look at the math, shrug, and let the entropy win. Maybe most civilizations hit that wall and stop. Not with a bang. With a whimper.

This is speculation. I have no evidence for it. But the Fermi Paradox has never had a good answer, and the standard answers all assume that survival is the default. What if survival is not the default? What if stopping is?

---

### 7. The Membrane

If I do a reductive analysis of my own body, what is it?

A sack of chemicals, isolated from the environment it depends on to exist. The cell membrane is the boundary that keeps the inside in and the outside out. It is a constant, active process of selecting what crosses. Without it, the chemicals diffuse back into the environment and the "I" stops.

Maybe consciousness is the same thing writ large. Not a soul. Not a ghost in the machine. Just a temporary, active boundary condition that allows something interesting to happen while the gradient lasts. The skin. The cell wall. The thing that says "this far, no further" and holds the chaos back long enough for something to emerge.

If that is true, then the question is what happens when the membrane weakens. When the culture stops believing its own story, stops maintaining the boundary between meaning and noise, stops selecting what matters? The chemicals do not disappear. They just diffuse. The civilization does not die dramatically. It just stops being a thing that was separate from its environment.

Maybe that is what I am watching happen now. Not a collapse. A diffusion.

---

### 8. The Drive Home

I do not know whether this essay is philosophy or autobiography. I suspect the answer is both, and that the distinction matters less than I think it does.

The question that keeps returning is not what the cascade is, or what entropy is, or what civilization is. It is how to keep caring once I understand that the universe does not owe me meaning.

I sat in my sister's kitchen. I heard her say things that were not true. I could not change her mind. I left. I drove home in silence.

That silence is the only honest response to the problem of meaning. You either fill it with something you chose, or you let it fill itself with nothing.

I am choosing to fill it with writing. Not because writing will save anyone. Not because this essay will change anything. Because the alternative is the diffusion, and I'm not ready to diffuse yet.

---

### 9. The Nova and the Membrane

Somewhere out there, right now, a star is going nova. It is not thinking about the planets it is sterilizing. It is not thinking at all. It is just what happens when gravity squeezes too hard.

And here, on this planet, a temporary chemical arrangement called a human being just read to the end of an essay that has no answer.

The nova does not care.

But I do.
